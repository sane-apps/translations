Eustathius of Antioch (c. 320–337) wrote this treatise to refute philosophical teachings about the soul. He addresses followers of Pythagoras and Plato who claimed that the soul is unbegotten, exists before the body, and drinks from a water of forgetfulness before birth. Eustathius argues instead that the soul is created and develops together with the body.

The argument begins by establishing that God fills all things and is not confined by place or subject to fixed laws and times. Eustathius then attacks the myth of the water of forgetfulness with a dilemma. If the philosophers escaped the drink of deception, then many others obviously did too, so the myth fails to explain universal ignorance. If they drank the water along with everyone else, they could not remember it well enough to fabricate myths about souls. Either way they are ignorant.

A reader will meet Pythagoreans and Platonists addressed directly as opponents, and David cited as a psalmist for the teaching that God is present in every place. Eustathius argues that newborn infants lack mind, reason, and wisdom, and only gradually acquire them as the body grows. He concludes that the soul grows with the body, and that defects of the soul correspond to defects of bodily organs, just as the first-formed human body lay lifeless until God breathed into it.
