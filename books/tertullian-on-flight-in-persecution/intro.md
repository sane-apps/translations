Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220), a Christian writer from Carthage, wrote this treatise to a brother in the faith named Fabius. Fabius had asked him whether Christians should flee during persecution. Tertullian answers with a full argument, prompted both by the question and by the increasing frequency of persecutions in his time.

The occasion is a practical and urgent one. Some believers were avoiding persecution by running away or by paying money to escape. Tertullian argues that persecution comes from God, not ultimately from the devil, because God uses it to test and prove faith. Since persecution is God's will and is good, it should neither be avoided nor can it be escaped. Flight is therefore wrong. The command to flee from city to city was given only to the apostles for a limited time and purpose, and no longer applies. Church leaders especially must not flee, and Christians must not buy their way out of persecution, since Christ has already purchased them with his blood.

A reader will meet Fabius, the brother who asked the question, and Rutilius, a martyr who repeatedly fled and paid to avoid danger, yet was still arrested and killed. Tertullian quotes and argues against objections from Fabius and an unnamed objector. He also quotes the Lord, Paul, John, Moses, and a saying of the Spirit. Key terms include persecution, testing, rejection, flight, buying one's way out, martyrdom, confession, denial, the Comforter, cowardice, shepherd, flock, money, extortionist, and betrayal. Bible books are cited under their old names: Genesis for Genesis, Apocalypsis for Revelation, and the prophets by their Septuagint names such as Zacharias, Ezechiel, and Hieremias. Psalm numbering follows the Hebrew numbering used in most modern English Bibles.
