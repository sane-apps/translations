Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for a Christian congregation hearing the Gospel read. He preaches on the opening of John's Gospel, which has just been read, to arm his hearers against arguments that the Son is a creature who once did not exist.

Basil urges his hearers not to admire John's opening words from a distance but to grasp their power, showing that in the beginning excludes any time before the Word and was refutes those who say the Son once was not. He explains Word as teaching that the Son comes forth from the Father without passion, as a mind's word expresses the whole mind, and reads and the Word was with God as preserving the distinct reality of Father and Son and the Word was God as confessing full divinity, leaving the four phrases as a fixed defense for faith. The reader meets John whose prologue is expounded, the Word with the Father, the Holy Spirit who speaks through the Gospel, the devil as thief of teaching, and the Apostle cited for the tongues of angels, while the opponents are quoted with he says and they say and If he was begotten, he was not, alongside words introduced as Proverbs said and as from the Apostle.

To follow it, read beginning as the unique beginning beyond which nothing can be thought, Word as the Son so named for his coming forth without passion, was as eternal existence without beginning, and person as distinct reality. Basil quotes the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament and cites Proverbs and 1 Corinthians, with a saying about the tongues of men and of angels given only as from the Apostle, who is not named further. Modern citations use standard English Bible names, noting Septuagint numbering where it differs.
