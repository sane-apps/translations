Severianus of Gabala (fl. c. 400) wrote these comments on the Letter to the Hebrews for Christian readers, especially those concerned with the authorship and interpretation of that book. He was bishop of Gabala in Syria and a popular preacher in Constantinople in the early fifth century. The work survives only in fragments quoted in later catena and scholia collections.

The occasion for the commentary was a dispute over whether Paul wrote Hebrews. Severianus defends Pauline authorship, arguing that Paul omitted his name because he was hated by Jews as teaching apostasy from the law, and did not want his name to hinder the letter's benefit. He explains the unusual style by saying the letter was written in Hebrew and translated by one of the disciples, by Luke, or rather by Clement. He also responds to heretics who used the letter's opening to deny the Son's eternal divine nature.

A reader will meet Paul, whose words from Hebrews and Galatians are quoted, along with John the evangelist, whose language about the Word Paul is said to imitate. Severianus also quotes the Lord's preaching of repentance and paraphrases Jeremiah. The commentary expounds the opening chapters to show that the Son is eternal, begotten from the Father's substance, and superior to angels while also truly human. It emphasizes the superiority of Christ's priesthood according to the order of Melchizedek over the Levitical priesthood, and warns against returning from Christian baptism and repentance to the observances of the law. Key terms include radiance, exact imprint of his being, priesthood, baptisms, repentance from dead works, dead works, camp, burden, sin that easily entangles, festal gathering, firstborn, tabernacle, and without genealogy. The author cites the Greek Old Testament and Greek New Testament, and the name Jesus son of Nave refers to Joshua.
