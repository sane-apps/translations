Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote the Homily on Thanksgiving as a homily preaching on Paul's command to the Thessalonians to rejoice always, pray without ceasing, and give thanks in everything. It is addressed to no named person. He wrote to answer objections that such commands are impossible amid life's sorrows.

The argument is that Christian joy rests not on outward circumstances but on God's gifts and the hope of eternal goods, so the command to rejoice always can be kept. Basil presents the tears of the saints and of Christ as measured and instructive and compatible with abiding joy, and urges restrained grief without theatrical mourning, with the mind fixed on heavenly promises and resurrection. The reader will meet Paul, Jesus, Job, David, Jonathan, Saul, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Lazarus. Paul, Job, Jesus, and other scriptural voices are quoted directly, while unnamed objectors who say the command is impossible are quoted and argued against.

To follow it, a reader should know that thanksgiving means thanksgiving to God, joy means abiding joy rooted in God and hope, grief means sorrow from affliction, and hope means hope in future goods promised by God. Command renders the apostolic command to rejoice always, citizenship renders belonging to heaven to which Paul summons believers, and tears, sufferings, resurrection, blessed, and moderation are used in their scriptural senses. Biblical books named in Greek forms appear here under standard English names, and Psalms follow the Septuagint numbering, which can differ from the Hebrew numbering.
