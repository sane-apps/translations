Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220) wrote this treatise for Christians in Carthage who were trying to earn a living and take part in public life without falling into idolatry. He was a Christian teacher in that city, and he wrote in the early third century to warn believers that idolatry is far broader than offering sacrifice to idols. His concern was that many Christians excused their involvement in trades, teaching, public office, military service, festivals, and oaths connected with pagan religion, and he wanted to show that such participation compromises the faith.

The occasion is the daily life of Christians in a society saturated with pagan worship. Tertullian argues that idolatry is the chief sin of the human race and includes every other major crime. It is not limited to sacrificing to idols but extends to making or decorating idols, teaching pagan literature, astrology, trading in goods used for idol worship, holding public office with its idolatrous trappings, military service, celebrating pagan festivals, and even swearing or being blessed by pagan gods. He insists that Christians must avoid every contact with idolatry, even at the cost of livelihood or social standing, because faith in God cannot be shared with demons.

A reader will meet Old Testament figures such as Enoch, Isaiah, Moses, Daniel, Joseph, and Joshua, as well as New Testament figures such as John the Baptist, Peter, Matthew, James, and John the Apostle. Tertullian quotes the prophets, the Psalms, the apostle Paul, and sayings of the Lord, and he argues against objectors who defend their practices. He also mentions the heretical group called Marcionites, who accused God of inconsistency between the law and later commands. Key terms include idolatry for any service of idols, demons for the unclean spirits behind idols, pomp of the devil for the display of worldly power renounced at baptism, and sacred commitment for the Christian pledge of faith. Old Testament books appear under their Greek and Latin names, such as Esaias for Isaiah and Moyses for Moses, and Psalm numbering follows the Septuagint.
