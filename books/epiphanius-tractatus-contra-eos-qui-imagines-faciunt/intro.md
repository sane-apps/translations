Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 310/320–403) was bishop of Salamis, the chief city of Cyprus, from 367 until his death. This treatise is attributed to him and belongs to a small group of writings against images that circulated under his name, though modern scholars debate whether all of them are genuine. It is addressed to Christians who make and honor painted images of Christ, the Mother of God, martyrs, angels, and prophets.

The work opposes the practice of making painted images for memorial and honor, which the author regards as a return to idolatry. He argues that making images of the saints dishonors rather than honors them, since Scripture promises they will be transformed into glory like Christ and the angels, not depicted in dead, voiceless matter.

The fathers at the Council of Laodicea are quoted pronouncing an anathema on those who abandon the church to invoke angels. The author calls Christ incomprehensible and uncircumscribable, meaning he cannot be grasped by the mind or contained in a visual form. The word images renders painted likenesses, worship renders the act of bowing before them, and idolatrous custom renders the practice the author opposes. Old Testament quotations follow the Greek Septuagint book names and Psalm numbering.
