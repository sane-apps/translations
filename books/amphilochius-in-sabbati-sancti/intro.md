Amphilochius of Iconium (c. 340–c. 395) was a fourth-century bishop in central Asia Minor, a friend of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus, and a participant in the First Council of Constantinople in 381. He wrote this homily for the Christians of his diocese, to be delivered during the Holy Saturday liturgy, the day when the church commemorates the burial of Christ and waits for his resurrection. His purpose was to turn the congregation from grief at the tomb toward confidence in the victory hidden within it, and to answer the mockery of both Jews and pagans who saw the crucifixion as proof that the Christian hope was dead.

The sermon was preached on the Great and Holy Saturday of the Paschal week, the day between Good Friday and Easter. Amphilochius addresses a congregation assembled for the burial feast of the Savior, and he speaks directly to two groups of opponents: Jews, who sealed the tomb and set guards over it, and Greeks, pagans who mock the Christian hope and have shut up their own hopes in tombs. He quotes the prophet David, Christ on the cross, and Christ's own words about the temple, and he argues against the Jewish precautions at the tomb, saying that the very act of sealing and guarding it shows they feared Jesus was no mere man.

A reader should know that the homily uses Hades for the realm of the dead, which Christ enters to despoil and fill with light, and that death and life are spoken of as persons, with death swallowing life but unable to hold it. The author quotes the Psalms according to the Septuagint numbering, so Psalm 15 in the sermon is Psalm 16 in modern English Bibles, and Psalm 48 is Psalm 49. He also quotes Isaiah, the Gospel of John, and Luke, and he alludes to the raising of Lazarus as proof of Christ's power over death. The sermon ends by inviting the congregation to join Mary and her companions at the tomb in faith and to make their words their own.
