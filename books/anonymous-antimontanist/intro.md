This treatise was written by an anonymous Christian author in the late second century. He addressed it to Avircius Marcellus, who had long urged him to write against the heresy associated with Miltiades, meaning the Montanists. The author delayed out of caution, but after encountering the church at Ancyra in Galatia troubled by the new prophecy, he debated the Montanists there and now sends a written account.

The author argues that the so-called new prophecy of Montanus and his female associates is a false prophecy, not the true prophetic gift promised by Christ. He shows that its origin was demonic, marked by ecstatic frenzy and love of preeminence, and that its prophets suffered no persecution and died shamefully. He refutes their appeal to martyrs and to prophetic succession, and insists that true prophecy never operates through ecstatic madness. The Montanist claim fails because no successors to Montanus and the women can be shown, though the prophetic gift should continue in the church until the final coming.

A reader will meet Montanus, the founder of the new prophecy, said to have fallen into ecstatic frenzy in Ardabau, and Maximilla, one of the two women filled with the false spirit. The author quotes a saying of Maximilla from a work directed against Asterius Urbanus, and he also quotes the words of that work's author. He mentions bishops Zoticus from the village of Comana and Julian from Apamea, who tried to test the spirit speaking through Maximilla, and Themison, a Montanist whose followers silenced those bishops. He cites New Testament prophets such as Agabus, Judas, Silas, and Philip's daughters as examples of true prophecy without ecstasy. The Montanists are called Cataphrygians, from their origin in Phrygia.
