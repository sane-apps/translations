Dionysius was bishop of Corinth in the mid-second century. He wrote pastoral letters to churches and individuals across the Greek-speaking world, including Rome, Sparta, Athens, Nicomedia, Gortyn in Crete, Amastris in Pontus, Cnossus, and a Christian woman named Chrysophora. He wrote to address local needs, encourage sound teaching, and respond to requests from fellow believers.

The letters survive only in fragments quoted by the church historian Eusebius. Dionysius wrote in a period when his letters carried enough authority that heretical groups tried to alter them by adding or removing material. He himself warns that his writings had been corrupted by agents of the devil. The fragments show his wide pastoral concern for orthodoxy, peace, and moral discipline across many churches.

A reader will meet Soter, bishop of Rome, praised for his generosity in sending aid to needy churches and to Christians condemned to forced labor in the mines. Dionysius recalls reading the Romans' letter publicly on the Lord's day and says the Corinthians will keep reading it, just as they do the earlier letter written through Clement. He praises the Romans and Corinthians for blending together the planting made by the apostles Peter and Paul, who taught together in Italy and were martyred at the same time. He also mentions Publius, a leader of the Athenian church who was martyred, and Quadratus, bishop of Athens after him. Dionysius the Areopagite, a member of the Athenian council converted by Paul according to the Acts of the Apostles, appears as the first bishop of Athens. Other figures include Philip, bishop of Gortyn, and Pinytus, bishop of Cnossus, whom Dionysius urged not to impose too heavy a burden of purity on the brethren. Bacchylides and Elpistus are two men who urged Dionysius to write to the church at Amastris. The fragments also refer to the heresy of Marcion, a false teaching judged by the standard of truth.
