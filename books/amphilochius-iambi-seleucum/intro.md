Amphilochius of Iconium (c. 340–c. 395) wrote this poem as a letter to a young man named Seleucus, whom he addresses as his son. Amphilochius was a bishop in Cappadocia, and he composed these verses to answer Seleucus' question about which books should be regarded as inspired Scripture. The poem combines fatherly moral guidance with doctrinal teaching and a catalogue of the biblical canon.

Seleucus had asked Amphilochius which writings carried divine authority, and the bishop responded with a didactic poem that first urges the young man to pursue virtue rather than unstable wealth. He warns him against the theater, beast-fights, and the hippodrome, and against corrupting company, quoting the apostle Paul's saying that bad company ruins good morals. After this moral instruction, Amphilochius turns to the Scriptures themselves, urging Seleucus to hold a middle way in Trinitarian doctrine between the errors of Sabellius, who confused the persons of the Trinity, and Arius, who divided the divine substance.

A reader should know that the poem uses Septuagint book names, so Esdras means Ezra, Paraleipomena means Chronicles, Osee means Hosea, and similar older forms appear for the prophets. The author distinguishes accepted writings from disputed or spurious ones, and he excludes several deuterocanonical books and the Revelation of John from the canon. Key terms include character for a person's moral way of life, inspired for writings breathed by God, and person for the individual subsistence within the Trinity, which must be neither confused nor divided.
