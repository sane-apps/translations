Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote these homilies for his congregation at Caesarea in Cappadocia. He delivered them to explain the Genesis creation account, counter pagan philosophical and heretical readings, and lead his hearers from visible creation to knowledge of the Creator.

Basil expounds Genesis 1:1–26 in nine homilies. He insists the world had a beginning and was made by God, not by chance or from eternal matter. He explains each day's work: light, firmament, gathered waters, vegetation, heavenly bodies, water creatures and birds, and land animals. Throughout, he draws moral lessons from nature and argues that the plural 'Let us make man' reveals the Father speaking to the Son.

A reader will meet Moses, Paul, Isaiah, Solomon, Jacob, Job, Daniel, Jeremiah, and Elisha, whose words Basil quotes. He also mentions the followers of Marcion and Valentinus, the Manichaeans, and the kings Sesostris and Darius the Mede. Basil quotes the Septuagint and follows its book names and Psalm numbering, so LXX Psalm 18 corresponds to Hebrew Psalm 19. Key terms include 'made' for God's deliberate creating, 'firmament' for the expanse separating waters, 'lights' for the sun and moon, 'living creature' for animals, 'according to kind' for fixed natural kinds, 'invisible and unformed' for the initial earth, 'the deep' for the covering water, 'Spirit of God' for the Holy Spirit active in creation, 'image' and 'likeness' for human creation and moral progress, and 'astrology' for the false art Basil refutes.
