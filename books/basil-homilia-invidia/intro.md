Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for a Christian congregation addressed as "brothers" to warn them against envy, which he presents as a destructive passion that makes a person an ally of the devil and an enemy of God and neighbor.

Preaching against envy in his congregation, Basil argues that envy is the most destructive passion of the soul because it consumes the envious person from within while doing little harm to its object, tracing it from the devil through Cain, Saul, Joseph's brothers and the Jews who crucified Christ as the root of murder and opposition to God, and then showing how envious people misrepresent virtues as vices and feed on others' misfortunes before urging the remedy of treating worldly goods as instruments for virtue and replacing envy with kindness and mutual forgiveness. The reader will meet Cain, Saul, David, Goliath, Joseph, Pharaoh, Paul and Solomon, and will hear quoted words from Paul on envy and murder and on mutual kindness, from Solomon on envy, from an imagined confession of the envious person, and from the women singing victory songs about David and Saul.

Here envy renders two Greek terms for grief at a neighbor's prosperity, envious person renders the one who resents another's good, and other key terms are rendered as the devil, virtue, prosperity, fighting against God, hypocrisy, conceited and demons. Basil quotes the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) and the New Testament, cited as Romans 1:29, Galatians 5:26 and Proverbs 14:30, with LXX noted where the wording differs, since the Greek of Proverbs 14:30 reads as a man's envy comes from his companion. The shame over Goliath refers to Saul's shame removed by David's defeat of the Philistine champion, after which the women's song comparing David and Saul provoked Saul's envy.
