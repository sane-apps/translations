Eustathius of Antioch (c. 320–337) wrote this treatise to a man named Eutropius, who had asked for his judgment on the story of the woman of Endor in 1 Samuel. Eutropius was dissatisfied with Origen's treatment of the passage, and Eustathius, as bishop of Antioch, wrote to refute Origen's claim that the soul of the prophet Samuel was truly summoned by the woman. The work is the only complete writing by Eustathius that survives.

The occasion is a dispute over biblical interpretation. Origen had argued that the figure Saul encountered through the woman of Endor was genuinely the deceased prophet Samuel. Eustathius responds that the apparition was a demon impersonating Samuel, since demons have no power to summon souls from Hades. He contends that the woman's words are unreliable because she was possessed, and that the apparition merely repeated prophecies Samuel had already spoken during his life. He further argues that Origen's reading blasphemes the Holy Spirit by attributing the possessed woman's words to Scripture's own voice, and that Origen's allegorical method is inconsistent, since he allegorizes Scripture elsewhere but treats this demonic episode as literal.

A reader should know that the Greek word translated here as 'medium' literally means 'belly-talker,' and that Eustathius uses it for the woman of Endor and for other forbidden practitioners condemned in Scripture. The book names follow the Septuagint: 'the first of Kingdoms' is 1 Samuel, 'the third of Kingdoms' is 1 Kings, and 'the fourth of Kingdoms' is 2 Kings. Eustathius quotes Origen's own words at length, and the reader will meet Saul, Samuel, David, Jonathan, Elijah, Micaiah, Ahab, Paul, Silas, Lazarus, Abraham, Caiaphas, Manasseh, Josiah, Isaiah, and Moses, as well as the philosophers Plato, Hesiod, and Homer.
