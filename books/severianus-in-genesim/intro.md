Severianus of Gabala (fl. c. 400) was bishop of Gabala in Syria and a well-known preacher in Constantinople. He delivered this homily to a Christian congregation as a sermon on Genesis 1:31, defending the goodness of all created things. His purpose was to show that animals used in Scripture as images of vice are not evil by nature, and to urge his hearers to value the divine words above all pleasures.

The sermon takes as its starting point the verse in which God saw all that he had made and declared it very good. Severianus argues that the virtues restore in us the original beauty of the image and likeness of God, while imitating the vices of irrational animals dishonors our rational freedom. Yet the animals themselves are not evil, and Scripture itself invites us to imitate their useful qualities, such as the ant's diligence, the bee's craftsmanship, the lion's courage, and the serpent's prudence. The same animal image can signify virtue or vice depending on how Scripture applies it, so readers must carefully distinguish the meanings.

A reader will meet God the Creator, Christ the Savior, the patriarch Jacob and his brother Esau, the psalmist David, the apostle Peter, and the devil. Scripture is quoted directly with formulas such as "Scripture says," "the Proverbist says," "David says," or "the prophet says." Christ is quoted with "the Savior says to the apostles" and "Christ the one who said." Jacob is quoted with "what does he say to Esau?" Key terms include piety, virtue, image of God, likeness, irrational animals, excellent quality, prudent, innocent, prudence, evildoer, honeycomb, divine words, and diligence. The homily cites the Septuagint, using its book names and Psalm numbering, so modern citations follow standard English Bible names and numbering with Septuagint differences noted where relevant.
