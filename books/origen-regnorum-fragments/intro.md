Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 254) wrote these fragments as part of his commentary on the first book of Kingdoms, known today as 1 Samuel. He was a Christian teacher and biblical scholar who produced commentaries on nearly every book of Scripture, and these surviving pieces come from larger works preserved in later collections. He wrote to explain difficult passages and to show how the narrative of Israel's early monarchy points to Christ and the spiritual life.

The fragments address passages that troubled readers, especially where God is said to be angry or to change his mind. Origen argues that such language describes God's just punishment and his providential plan, not human emotions. He also defends God's justice in episodes like the defeat of Israel, the rejection of Saul, and the execution of Saul's descendants, showing that sin and impiety brought these judgments.

A reader will meet Eli the priest, Samuel the prophet, Saul the first king, and David, whom Origen treats as a type of Christ uniting priesthood and kingship. The book is called the first of Kingdoms, which is 1 Samuel in modern Bibles, and Psalm numbers follow the Septuagint, often one lower than the Hebrew. Key terms include God's anger for his punishment of sinners, change of plan for his shift from one providential arrangement to another, and plan for his ordering of events. The Philistines are the foreign enemies of Israel, the ark of the covenant is the sign of God's presence, and the bread of the presence is the holy bread David ate.
