Macarius the Egyptian (fl. 4th/5th c.) was a Coptic monk and hermit who lived in the desert of Scetis and became one of the most influential spiritual teachers of early monasticism. These homilies are traditionally attributed to him, though scholars now often call the author Pseudo-Macarius because the final form cannot be proved to be his own. They were written to exhort Christians to pursue the indwelling of the Holy Spirit in this life, so that the hidden glory of the soul may be revealed in the body at the resurrection.

The homilies address Christians who are tempted to live like people of the world, bound to earthly things. Macarius insists that true Christians are a different kind of people, with different conduct, mind, word, and action, because they long for the love of the heavenly Father alone. He urges them to strive through faith and virtuous living to acquire now the heavenly garment, since whatever glory the soul stores within will be revealed outwardly in the body on the day of resurrection.

A reader will meet Solomon, the biblical king cited from the Gospels as an example of earthly glory that cannot match the lilies, and the Lord, Jesus Christ, whose saying about Solomon is quoted directly. The author uses the image of spring, when trees put forth leaves, flowers, and fruits after winter, as a type of the resurrection. He says that for true Christians the resurrection day is like the first month, Xanthicus, called April, when the glory of the Holy Spirit hidden in their souls will come forth and clothe their bodies. The sun of righteousness is a biblical title for Christ, whose power brings forth this hidden glory.
