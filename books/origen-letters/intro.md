Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 254) wrote these two letters to different correspondents. The first is addressed to Julius Africanus, a Christian chronographer who had written to Origen questioning the authenticity of the story of Susanna and other passages found in the Greek text of Daniel but absent from the Hebrew. The second is addressed to Gregory, Origen's former student, later known as Gregory Thaumaturgus, and was written about 235 CE after Origen had fled persecution to Cappadocia while Gregory had gone to Egypt.

In the letter to Africanus, Origen defends the Church's use of the Greek form of Daniel, including Susanna, Bel and the Dragon. He argues that the Jews have removed passages that cast blame on elders and rulers, and that the Church is right to retain them. He shows that differences between Hebrew and Greek copies exist in many biblical books, including Daniel, Tobit, Jeremiah, and Genesis, and that Jewish traditions themselves preserve the substance of the disputed stories. He urges that the Church should not discard texts that have edified the churches, even if they are absent from the Hebrew. In the letter to Gregory, he encourages his former pupil to devote his natural ability to Christianity, using Greek philosophy and the liberal arts as preparatory studies for interpreting Scripture, and to read Scripture with prayer, knocking and seeking.

Key terms include "hidden books" for books or passages kept hidden or removed from public reading, "copies" for differing manuscripts of the biblical text, "elders" for the leaders in the Susanna story, and "traditions" for Jewish traditional teachings passed down outside the public biblical books. In the letter to Gregory, "liberal arts" and "preparatory studies" refer to the general learning that prepares a student for Scripture, and "plunder the Egyptians" describes the allegorical taking of useful materials from pagan learning. Origen also speaks of the "hidden meaning of Scripture" and of "knocking" on the closed door of Scripture's meaning. , and the Greek form of Daniel includes passages not found in the Hebrew.
