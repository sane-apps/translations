Marcellus of Ancyra (c. 280–c. 374), bishop of Ancyra, wrote this treatise against opponents who misuse Scripture to deny the full divinity of the Son. They ask how the Son can be like the Father or from the Father's essence, given texts about receiving life, being raised, being forsaken, and not knowing the last day. Marcellus responds that these humble sayings belong to Christ's assumed humanity, not to his eternal divinity.

The opponents bring forward the human sayings of the Son's poverty to establish their own blasphemy. Marcellus argues that the Son is by nature equal and consubstantial with the Father, and that the Father acts through him in raising, sanctifying, and glorifying the incarnate Lord. He also insists that the Holy Spirit is uncreated, of the same divinity and authority as Father and Son, and that Scripture attributes the same works to all three, including creating, sanctifying, raising, giving life, speaking in prophets, and filling all things.

A reader will meet many biblical voices quoted and argued against: Paul, John, Isaiah, David, Peter, Thomas, Philip, Stephen, Mary, Adam, Gabriel, Ananias, Sapphira, Barnabas, Agabus, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Daniel, Moses, John the Baptist, Luke, Matthew, Timothy, and Satan. Key terms include 'poverty' for the voluntary lowliness the Son took on in becoming human, 'flesh' for his incarnate human nature, 'plan of salvation' for the divine arrangement by which the Son became human, 'consubstantial' for being of the same substance as the Father, 'firstfruits' for Christ's body as the first portion of the Church, 'form of a servant' for the humble condition of his incarnate life, 'adoption' for the gift by which human beings become children of God, 'Mother of God' for Mary, 'Lord of hosts' for the divine title applied to all three persons, 'person' for the distinct reality of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, 'guarantee' for the Holy Spirit as pledge of the inheritance, 'Church' for Christ's body, and 'mediator' for the incarnate Christ as both God and man.
