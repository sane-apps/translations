Augustine of Hippo (354–430) wrote this polemical psalm and song for ordinary Christians, especially those attracted to or troubled by the Donatist party, to persuade them to remain in Catholic unity and to answer Donatist claims about the origins of their schism.

He urges those who rejoice in peace to judge the controversy truly, retelling how Donatist leaders broke the net of the church by setting up altar against altar after failing to prove that Caecilian's ordainer had handed over the scriptures. He argues the church remains a mixed net of good and bad fish until the final separation at the shore, and a threshing floor of wheat and chaff, so sinners must be tolerated as Christ tolerated Judas, and he points to the succession of bishops from the see of Peter as the rock against which the gates of hell cannot prevail while calling Donatists back to the vine and to peace. The reader will meet Donatus, for whom the party is named, Caecilian the bishop of Carthage, his accusers Botrus and Caelestius, the imperial official Macarius, and Judas, Peter, John the Baptist and Ezekiel. Besides Augustine's own voice, Mother Church is heard speaking to her children and a hypothetical catholic Christian is quoted asking why he should be rebaptized, both set against a Donatist interlocutor whose claim to rebaptize is argued against.

To follow it, note that net means the church gathering good and bad until the end, threshing floor with wheat and chaff pictures the same mixture, altar against altar means schism, traitors means those accused of handing over the scriptures, see means the episcopal seat with Peter's see as the rock, vine means Christ and the church, and rebaptize refers to the Donatist practice toward Catholics. Botrus and Caelestius appear as enemies of Caecilian in the original accusation, Macarius as an imperial official whose violence Donatists blamed on Catholics, Donatus as the leader who gives the party its name, and circumcellions as violent Donatist partisans. Psalms are cited by modern Hebrew numbering and Bible books by modern names where the source uses older forms.
