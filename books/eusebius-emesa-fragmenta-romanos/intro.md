Eusebius of Emesa (c. 300–c. 359) was a Christian bishop in Syria, educated in Edessa, Antioch, and Alexandria, and a student of Eusebius of Caesarea. He wrote this commentary to explain difficult passages in Paul's Letter to the Romans, especially Romans 7, for readers who found Paul's confessions there puzzling. His approach follows the Antiochene tradition of literal and historical interpretation.

The work addresses a problem in Romans 7, where Paul seems to say that he does the evil he does not want to do. Eusebius argues that Paul speaks there in an assumed voice, not in his own person, to refute people who love sin and blame the flesh for evil. A reader will meet Paul, Adam, the devil, and Christ, and will hear the words of Paul and Adam quoted and argued against.

To follow the argument, a reader should know that 'sin' in this passage means the devil, not a substance or the human flesh, and that the law and commandment remain holy, just, and good. The flesh itself is not evil by nature; rather, the devil dwelling in sinners is the agent of evil. Christians buried with Christ in baptism have been freed from this 'sin' and from condemnation. The term 'Beliar' is another name for the devil, also called Satan, the serpent, and the lion.
