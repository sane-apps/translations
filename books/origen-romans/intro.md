Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 254) wrote this commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans around 246 CE, while in Caesarea. He was the most important theologian and biblical scholar of the early Greek church, and this is the oldest surviving commentary on Romans. He wrote it to defend human freedom against Gnostic teachers who used Paul's letter to argue that each person's deeds are caused not by purpose but by an unalterable nature received at birth.

The work was originally composed in Greek, but only fragments of that text survive. The complete form available today comes from a Latin translation made around 406 CE by Rufinus of Aquileia, an admirer of Origen. Origen opens with a prayer to God who teaches man knowledge, and he notes that Romans is considered more difficult than Paul's other letters because of its sometimes unclear expressions and the many questions it raises, especially those used by heretics to deny free will.

A reader will meet Paul, whose words from Romans are quoted directly and explained, along with Jesus Christ, Abraham, Isaac, and David. Origen treats God's impartial judgment on Jews and Greeks, the true circumcision of the heart, justification through faith in Christ's blood, baptism as burial with Christ into death, and the inner conflict between the law of the mind and the law of sin. He argues that not all who are from Israel are Israelites, and that election depends on God's mercy, not human will or effort. Key terms include faith, God's righteousness, the law, the Spirit, the flesh, grace, sin, death, adoption, baptism, and circumcision of the heart.
