Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220) wrote this short treatise to Christians in prison awaiting possible martyrdom. He was a Christian writer in Carthage, and the work likely dates from the early years of Roman persecution, around 197 AD, though some place it during the persecution of 202–203 AD. He wrote to encourage and strengthen imprisoned believers, offering spiritual nourishment to accompany the physical food supplied by the church.

The letter addresses Christians held in prison for their faith. Tertullian urges them to preserve peace and unity among themselves, since discord would give the devil a victory in his own house. He reframes the prison as a place of spiritual retreat rather than punishment, arguing that the world itself is a darker prison and that Christians have already renounced it. He presents their imprisonment as training for the spiritual contest of martyrdom, comparing it to the strict discipline of athletes and soldiers.

A reader will meet many pagan examples of endurance: Lucretia, Mucius, Heraclitus, Empedocles, Peregrinus, Dido, Hasdrubal's wife, Regulus, Cleopatra, and an unnamed Athenian courtesan. Tertullian argues that if pagans endured or even sought out bitter sufferings for earthly fame, through mental affliction, or through involuntary misfortune, Christians should gladly endure them for truth and salvation. Key terms include "martyrs," "prison," "spirit," "flesh," "world," "contest," "glory," "vanity," "retreat," "training ground," "prize," and "crown." He quotes the apostle Paul from 1 Corinthians and alludes to Matthew. The Lacedaemonians are the Spartans, and the tortoise formation is a Roman military maneuver in which soldiers locked shields together.
