Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for the congregation assembled for the martyr's feast. He preached it on the feast day of the martyr Barlaam. He preached to celebrate Barlaam's endurance under torture and his victory over fire.

Basil contrasts the old practice of mourning the dead with the Christian joy around the deaths of the saints, since death is a passage to better life. He presents Barlaam as a rustic countryman with a speech impediment and an athlete of piety who despised tortures and looked to the eternal prizes. The climax is the contest with fire, when his right hand was stretched out over a burning altar with incense in it and remained stretched out and unconquered while the persecutors hoped the fire would bend it and drop the incense. The reader will meet Barlaam, Christ for whom he suffers and who judges the contest, and Paul cited as one untrained in speech but not in knowledge. Basil gives words to the persecutors through their reasoning and puts the words of the Prophet and Psalmist into the martyr's mouth, and he speaks directly to Barlaam.

Here martyr means a witness who suffers and dies for Christ, piety means true religion toward God and impiety its opposite, and athlete, wrestler and wrestling match describe the contest of martyrdom, with crown and prize for victory. The right hand is the focus, with fire, altar, incense and demons marking the demand to offer incense to false gods, so letting the hand bend would have meant a drink offering to the demons, while the more blessed life is that better life after death. Psalm citations follow the Septuagint numbering, with modern numbers noted where they differ. At the end Basil calls on painters to depict the contest and gives glory to Christ.
