Eusebius of Emesa (c. 300–c. 359) was a Greek-speaking bishop in Syria, educated under Eusebius of Caesarea and known for his biblical commentaries. These fragments come from his explanation of Paul's Letter to the Galatians. They are not the fragments of Severianus of Gabala. He wrote for Christian readers who needed help understanding Paul's argument against imposing Jewish observances on Gentile believers.

The commentary addresses a crisis in the Galatian churches. Some from the circumcision party had gone out and persuaded Gentile believers to judaize, contrary to the apostolic decree and the Holy Spirit. Eusebius defends Paul's gospel of grace against those who would impose Jewish observances, insisting that Christ alone died for the world and that Paul's gospel, preached in the power of the Holy Spirit, sets believers outside the law.

A reader will meet Paul, whose words are quoted and argued from throughout, and Peter, whom Paul confronted at Antioch. Eusebius also discusses Moses, Ishmael, and Jeremiah. He argues that the law was given because of transgressions to train the mind and expose sin, but its bodily observances are dead for the believer. Baptism clothes all alike with Christ, making grace common to Jew and Greek, male and female. Paul's so-called allegory of Hagar and Sarah is a prophecy enacted through historical events, showing that Sinai and the present Jerusalem belong to slavery, while the heavenly citizenship brings freedom. Eusebius concludes that neither circumcision nor uncircumcision matters, but only the new creation in Christ. Key terms to follow include the law, grace, works of the law, faith, allegory, basic principles, slavery, freedom, new creation, circumcision, uncircumcision, Judaism, Christianity, and commandment. Old Testament books are cited by their Greek titles, such as the Song of Songs.
