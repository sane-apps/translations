Eusebius of Emesa (c. 300–c. 359) was a Greek-speaking bishop in Syria, a student of Eusebius of Caesarea, and a writer whose biblical commentaries survive mostly in fragments and translations. This work is a commentary on Paul's Letter to the Galatians, written to explain the apostle's argument against those who were persuading Gentile believers to adopt Jewish observances.

The commentary begins from the crisis Paul addresses in Galatians: some from the circumcision party went out and persuaded the nations to judaize, contrary to the apostolic decree and the Holy Spirit. Eusebius defends Paul's gospel of grace against those who would impose Jewish observances, insisting that Christ alone died for the world and that Paul's gospel, preached in the power of the Holy Spirit, sets believers outside the law.

A reader will meet Paul, Peter, Moses, and Ishmael. Paul's words from Galatians are quoted and explained. Eusebius argues that the law was given because of transgressions to train the mind and expose sin, but its bodily observances are dead for the believer. Baptism clothes all alike with Christ, making grace common to Jew and Greek, male and female. Paul's so-called allegory of Hagar and Sarah is a prophecy enacted through historical events, showing that Sinai and the present Jerusalem belong to slavery, while the heavenly citizenship brings freedom. Eusebius concludes that neither circumcision nor uncircumcision matters, but only the new creation in Christ. Old Testament books are cited by their Greek titles, such as the Song of Songs, which modern readers know as Song of Solomon.
