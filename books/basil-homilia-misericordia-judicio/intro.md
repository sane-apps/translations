Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for a Christian congregation whom he addresses as brothers.

He exhorts his hearers to combine mercy with just dealing, warning that almsgiving from unjust gain is unacceptable to God and that refraining from injustice without giving to the poor is also insufficient. He argues that true piety requires justice and mercy together, holds up Zacchaeus the tax collector as the model who repaid what was taken and gave half his goods to the poor, and teaches that mercy to the poor is service to Christ himself to be rewarded at the judgment. The reader will meet Isaiah quoted as teaching from the person of God, Malachi on tithes and offerings, and Paul on the godless mind and on working to have something to share, and will hear the words of God through Isaiah and Malachi, the words of the Lord Jesus, the words of the king to those on his right, and the answer of the righteous.

To follow it, it helps to know how central words are used here. Mercy means mercy shown especially to the poor and needy, justice means just judgment in acquiring and using wealth, almsgiving means concrete aid to the poor, and kindness means love for others expressed in generosity. The divine Apostle is Paul. Old Testament quotations follow the Septuagint and are cited by standard English names such as Proverbs, Isaiah and Malachi, with Psalms numbered as in the Septuagint, while New Testament quotations follow the Gospels and letters such as Matthew, Luke, Romans and Ephesians.
