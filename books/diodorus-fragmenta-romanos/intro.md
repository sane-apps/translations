Diodorus of Tarsus (c. 330–c. 390) wrote this commentary for Christian readers interpreting Paul's Letter to the Romans. He was a bishop of Tarsus, originally from Antioch, where he spent most of his early life and taught. He wrote to explain difficult passages in Romans, especially where Paul's language about Adam, the law, sin, election, and Israel could be misunderstood.

Diodorus reads Romans as a sustained argument that sin and death entered through Adam, while grace and life come through Christ. The Mosaic law was given as a temporary, educative measure that exposed sin but could not save; the natural law already made humans responsible. God's election is based on foreknowledge of each person's disposition, so Pharaoh, Esau, and Jacob were treated according to what God foresaw in them. Israel's fall served God's plan by bringing the nations to faith, yet a remnant of Israel will also be saved. The commentary closes with praise for the revelation of the Trinity.

A reader will meet Adam, whose disobedience brought death to all and who serves as a type of Christ; Moses, the lawgiver; Abraham, whose true children are those of the promise; Isaac, Jacob, Esau, and Pharaoh as examples of election based on foreknowledge; Elijah, whose seven thousand faithful men prefigure the remnant of Israel; and Eve, Cain, and Aaron in brief illustrations. Paul's words are quoted and argued against throughout, along with quotations from the Old Testament and imagined objections. Key terms include law, grace, sin, righteousness, faith, foreknowledge, free will, election, remnant, adoption, flesh, Spirit, righteous act, transgression, and plan. Old Testament citations follow the Greek text, and modern citations use standard English Bible names and numbering.
