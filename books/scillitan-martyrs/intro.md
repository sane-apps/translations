On 17 July AD 180, six Christians from Scillium in Numidia were brought into the hearing chamber at Carthage before Saturninus, proconsul of Africa: Speratus, Nartzalus, Cittinus, Donata, Secunda, and Vestia. Asked to swear by the emperor's genius and return to the Roman way, they refused. Speratus, who speaks for the group, says they have never done wrong, never cursed anyone, and give thanks when ill-treated; in their case, searched at the hearing, are books and letters of Paul.

The record is a court dialogue, not a later legend. The proconsul offers pardon, then thirty days to reconsider; the prisoners answer, in turn, "I am a Christian," "Honour to Caesar, as Caesar's due; but fear to God," "What I am, that I want to remain." The sentence is read from the tablet, twelve names are proclaimed by the herald, and all are beheaded the same day. It is the earliest dated Latin martyr act, and among the earliest evidence for a Christian book collection in North Africa.

This translation is made from the Latin printed by Oscar von Gebhardt in Acta martyrum selecta (Berlin, 1902), pages 92-96, where a Greek version of the same passio stands alongside; the Greek was used only to check the sense. Gebhardt's apparatus is recorded with the source text: the consul's name is read as Condianus with Usener, and no closing clause has been supplied where the Latin column ends.
