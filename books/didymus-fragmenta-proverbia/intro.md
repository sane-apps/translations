Didymus the Blind (c. 313–c. 398) was a Christian theologian in the Church of Alexandria, where he taught for about half a century. Blind from the age of four, he was a student of Origen and a leading opponent of Arianism. After his death he was condemned along with Origen and Evagrius Ponticus in connection with the Second Council of Constantinople in 553, and most of his works were therefore not copied during the European Middle Ages.

This commentary on Proverbs survives only in fragments preserved in catenae, or chain commentaries. It is important for understanding the history of Alexandrian theology and for characterizing Didymus as a writer. It is not Evagrius Ponticus's Notes on Proverbs, and it is not his Exposition on Proverbs. The work is not among the papyrus codices discovered near Tura, Egypt, in 1941, which greatly increased modern knowledge of Didymus but did not include the Proverbs commentary.

A reader will encounter allegorical interpretation of Proverbs, exegesis of Proverbs and the soul, the nature of wisdom and creation, the deceiver and the nature of good, the corruption of praise, the devil's methods and the soul's flight, and proverbial exegesis.
