Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220) wrote this treatise after he had left the Catholic Church and joined the New Prophecy movement. He addresses it to Catholic Christians who oppose that movement's fasting practices. His purpose is to defend the stricter fasts of the New Prophecy against critics he calls unspiritual Christians.

The occasion is a dispute over discipline. Tertullian's opponents accept fasting in principle but reject the New Prophecy because it teaches frequent fasting. Tertullian argues that fasting is not a novelty but is grounded in God's original command to Adam, in Israel's history, and in Christ's own example. He contends that those who oppose stricter fasting are driven by love of food and the belly, and that their objections from Scripture are misread.

A reader will meet Adam, Moses, Elijah, Daniel, Samuel, Hezekiah, Ahab, Hannah, Anna the prophetess, Cornelius, and Timothy as examples of fasting. Tertullian also quotes and argues against the words of his opponents, and he appeals to the Holy Spirit, whom he calls the Advocate, as the one who authorizes the New Prophecy's observances. He defends three practices: fasts, dry-food fasts, and station fasts, with the extension of station fasts until evening defended as part of the practice. The book names follow the Septuagint, so Leviticus, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Joel, and Joshua appear under their older forms. The term unspiritual Christians is Tertullian's label for mainstream Christians who live by the soul rather than the Spirit.
