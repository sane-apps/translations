Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220) wrote this treatise for fellow Christians in Carthage, especially recent converts and the baptized. He addresses believers who were still attending the public shows of the Roman world, and he writes to persuade them that such entertainments are incompatible with the faith they professed at baptism. He also answers pagan critics who claimed that enjoying these pleasures does not offend God.

The occasion is the growing habit of Christians in Carthage and elsewhere attending the circus, theater, stadium, and amphitheater. Tertullian argues that all these shows are rooted in idolatry and therefore belong to the devil, whom Christians renounced at baptism. He traces the origins, titles, equipment, places, and arts of the games to pagan gods and demon worship, and he insists that the passions aroused by the shows, such as fury, lust, vanity, and cruelty, are contrary to Christian discipline.

A reader will meet Tertullian himself as teacher, along with quoted voices: pagan objectors, Christians who demand explicit scriptural prohibition, King David, and the Apostle Paul. Tertullian quotes Psalm 1:1, Exodus 20:13, and 1 Corinthians 8:4 and 10:20-21. Key terms include idolatry, the devil's pomp, shows, renunciation, seal of faith, demons, desire, pleasure, circus, theater, stadium, amphitheater, gladiatorial show, games, and pagans. Old Testament books are cited by their Septuagint numbering, so Psalm 1 appears as the first voice of David.
