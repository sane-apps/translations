John Chrysostom (c. 349–407) wrote this homily for a congregation celebrating the feast of the martyr Romanus. The occasion was a festal gathering, and Chrysostom spoke to honor the martyr and to encourage the Christians present.

The homily opens by celebrating the martyrs as members of one body with the Church, using the image of martyrs as the head and Christians as the feet, so their victory is a shared joy. Chrysostom then praises love as greater even than martyrdom and asks why the devil had Romanus's tongue cut out rather than killing him outright. God overturned this scheme: after the tongue was removed, Romanus continued to speak by divine grace, with a spiritual voice greater than nature.

The apostle Paul is quoted repeatedly on love and sympathy, and Christ is quoted on the love that marks his disciples. David the psalmist is quoted on God's wonderful name and praise from infants' mouths. Chrysostom also cites Aaron's rod that blossomed when the priesthood was dishonored, comparing it to Romanus's mouth blossoming because Christ was blasphemed. He closes by urging the congregation to imitate Romanus's endurance and trust God's help. Key terms include martyr, martyrdom, love, tongue, voice, the devil, crown, endurance, resurrection, confession, grace, and Church.
