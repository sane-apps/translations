Didymus the Blind (c. 313–c. 398) was a Christian teacher in Alexandria who, despite losing his sight in early childhood, became one of the most learned biblical interpreters of his day. He remained a layman all his life and wrote this verse-by-verse commentary on Genesis for Christian readers seeking to understand both the literal narrative and its deeper spiritual meaning. The work likely comes from his teaching activity in Alexandria and may be his earliest surviving commentary.

The commentary survived only in fragments on papyrus codices discovered in 1941 at Tura, near Cairo. Didymus was later condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council in 553 for his defense of Origen, which contributed to the loss of most of his writings. In this work he reads Genesis as a divinely ordered account of creation, fall, and restoration, arguing that the six days are not chronological but arranged by divine wisdom and number-symbolism, and that visible creation points to invisible realities.

A reader will meet Adam, Eve, and the serpent, who represent mind, sense, and the devil in the allegorical reading, as well as Cain, Abel, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, and Hagar, each standing for a stage or type of the soul's progress. Didymus frequently quotes the apostle Paul as an authority for allegorical interpretation and cites the Jewish interpreter Philo for readings of names and numbers. The translation uses 'beginning' for the Greek word that can also mean first principle, 'Word' for the divine rational principle identified with the Son, and 'firmament' for the dividing structure in creation. Old Testament books follow modern English names, so 'the first of Kingdoms' appears as 1 Samuel, 'Jesus son of Nave' as Joshua, and 'the Song of Songs' as Song of Solomon. Psalm numbering follows the Septuagint.
