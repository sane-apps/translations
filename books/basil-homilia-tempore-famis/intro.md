Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for the Christian congregation of Caesarea to call them to repentance and almsgiving.

A severe drought and famine had struck the region, and Basil presents it as God's correction for sin, especially greed and neglect of the poor. He describes the devastation, contrasts shallow repentance with the mourning of the Ninevites, warns that hoarded wealth is useless in famine and that the unmerciful face judgment, and closes by urging generosity after Joseph and the early Christians and readiness of the soul as a bride for Christ. The reader meets Amos, Elijah, Job, Joseph, Habakkuk and the Israelite young man he feeds in the lions den in Babylon, Achan, Zimri, the historian Josephus, and the widow of Zarephath. The text quotes Amos, the poor man's model prayer, the widow, the Lord, and the Psalmist.

This translation follows the Septuagint in names and Psalm numbering, with modern citations in standard English form such as Amos 4:7-8 and Psalm 45:9-11 for LXX Psalm 44. Achar appears as Achan, Zambri as Zimri, Ambakoum as Habakkuk, the Sidonian widow as the widow of Zarephath, and the Babylonian pit as the lions den in Babylon. Famine and drought name the disaster, greed names the chief sin, almsgiving names concrete aid to the poor, and generous person and selfish person name those separated at the judgment. The Israelite young man is the young captive fed in the pit and is not named Daniel here, Habakkuk is the prophet carried to feed him, Pentephres is the Egyptian master whom Joseph served, Josephus is the Jewish historian cited on the siege famine, and Achan is the figure whose sacrilege brought punishment on Israel.
