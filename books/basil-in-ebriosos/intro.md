Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) preached this homily to his congregation on the day of the resurrection commemoration, when members of his flock, especially women, formed choruses at the martyrs' shrines before the city and turned the holy day into drunken dancing and lewd behavior.

Basil laments that one evening and one assault of the enemy have undone all his Lenten exhortations, describes drunkenness as a voluntary demon that destroys reason, makes people worse than animals, and ruins both soul and body, rebukes elaborate drinking parties and sexual shamelessness, warns that such behavior excludes people from God's kingdom, and closes by urging repentance through fasting, psalmody, tears, almsgiving, and separation from the unrepentant. The reader will meet Isaiah, quoted for his woe against drinkers, Jeremiah, cited as an example of compelled speech, Cain, whose curse of trembling is applied to drunkards, and Phinehas, whose reward is held out to the faithful, while short quotations from Scripture run through the argument and Basil turns to rebuke a drinker and a host in direct address.

To follow it, a reader needs only the renderings used here: strong drink means any intoxicating drink and not only wine, wine is spoken of as God's gift for the weak turned into a weapon of excess, lust means unrestrained shamelessness, hangover means the stupor after excess, and fasting and almsgiving are presented as remedies for sin. Martyrs' shrines are the places before the city where the choruses danced, Pentecost is the coming feast after the insulted Pascha, Cain's curse means trembling, Phinehas is held out as a biblical zealot, and Isaiah and Jeremiah appear in standard English form because Basil quotes the Septuagint and also quotes the Gospels.
