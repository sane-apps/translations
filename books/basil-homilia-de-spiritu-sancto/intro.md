This homily was written by Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), the preacher and archbishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia. No individual addressee is named. He writes to defend the divinity of the Holy Spirit and to ground this teaching in baptism, Scripture, and the Eucharist.

Basil argues that the Holy Spirit is not a creature but belongs to the divine Trinity, because baptism is into Father, Son, and Holy Spirit and because Scripture attributes the same divine activity to the Spirit as to the Father and the Son. He teaches that the Spirit sanctifies, reveals, gives life, and speaks with divine authority, warns against applying the name Son or generation to the Spirit, and connects this faith to baptism and the Eucharist through which believers are reborn and receive life. The reader will meet Paul, David the psalmist, Peter, John, and Jesus Christ, whose words are quoted, along with the words of the hearers, while Basil argues throughout against those who would rank the Spirit among created things.

In this translation Trinity means Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, creation means all things made by God, activity means their shared divine working, and other words are rendered as Advocate, anointing, sanctify, give life, sonship, guarantee, first fruits, baptism, Eucharist, and body and blood. Old book names are given in modern form, so Jesus son of Nave appears as Joshua and the first of Kingdoms as 1 Samuel. Psalms follow the Greek Old Testament numbering, so a citation gives the Hebrew number in parentheses as in Psalm 32:6 (Hebrew 33:6), and the phrase with the pair he completes the Trinity means with the Father and the Son.
