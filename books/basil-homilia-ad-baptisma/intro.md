Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) wrote this homily for unbaptized hearers, especially catechumens and those delaying baptism. He urges those who have long delayed to receive baptism at once, teaching that Easter is especially fitting because it is the memorial of the resurrection and baptism is power for resurrection.

Basil argues that every moment of life is the right time for baptism, and confronts the excuse of waiting until old age or after enjoying sin as a trick of the devil and a dangerous gamble with death. He presents baptism as freedom from sin, adoption as God's child, and the seal that marks a person as belonging to Christ, and urges immediate enrollment in the Church, warning that those who keep delaying may die unbaptized and face eternal loss. The reader will meet Solomon, quoted at the opening about times and seasons, John the Baptist, Elijah, the eunuch baptized at once after hearing the gospel from Philip, Cain, and the devil, with words of the Lord from Scripture set against imagined objections of the delaying hearer, imagined advice of the devil, and an imagined lament of a dying unbaptized person.

To follow it, it helps to know that baptism is also called rebirth and enlightenment, and is spoken of as repentance, grace, freedom, adoption, seal, enrollment, garment of light, and promise of the kingdom of heaven. Basil quotes the Septuagint and follows its book names and Psalm numbering, but citations here use standard English Bible book names and Hebrew Psalm numbering. The eunuch needs no other introduction here than as the man baptized at once after Philip preached the gospel to him.
