Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220) wrote this treatise for fellow Christians, whom he addresses as “blessed ones.” A Christian teacher in Carthage trained in Roman law, he composed the work around AD 192 to instruct believers in the meaning and practice of prayer. He offers a catechetical and disciplinary explanation of the Lord’s Prayer and of Christian prayer customs, correcting what he regards as superstitious or unscriptural habits that had arisen in the churches.

The treatise presents the Lord’s Prayer as a complete summary of the gospel and the model of all Christian prayer. Tertullian expounds each petition in turn, showing how it teaches faith, holiness, submission to God’s will, hope for the kingdom, dependence on Christ the daily bread, confession of sins, and deliverance from temptation and evil. He then addresses the conditions and customs of prayer: the need for peace with brethren, purity of heart, clean hands, modest posture and voice, the veiling of virgins, kneeling, times of prayer, and the kiss of peace. He concludes that Christian prayer is the spiritual sacrifice that has replaced the old offerings, and that it is powerful for every good work.

A reader will meet many biblical figures whose words and examples are quoted or argued against, including Jesus Christ, John the Baptist, Moses, Peter, Paul, Eve, Adam, Rebecca, Daniel, Isaiah, Jonah, Cain, Lamech, Joseph, and Pilate. Tertullian also cites Hermas, author of the Shepherd, and the martyrs under the altar from Revelation. To follow the work, know that “teaching” renders disciplina, the rule of Christian life and worship; “fast day” renders statio, a day of fasting and prayer drawn from military watch-duty; and “debts” renders debita, understood as sins requiring forgiveness. Old Testament books appear under their traditional names, with Psalm numbering following the Septuagint system.
