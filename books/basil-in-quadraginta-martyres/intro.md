Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) delivered this homily to the congregation assembled for the martyrs' feast on the commemoration of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste to honor their witness and move the gathered faithful to imitate their endurance.

Basil argues that honoring martyrs proves love for God and prepares the hearer for imitation. He presents the Forty as one soul in many bodies, soldiers of Christ who confessed boldly under threat, and narrates their trial by freezing in a lake, their mutual encouragement, the desertion of one man for the warm bath, and his replacement by the guard who saw heavenly rewards and confessed Christ. He closes by urging the congregation to seek the Forty as intercessors and models. The reader will meet the Forty, the hostile ruler who condemns them, the deserter, the guard, and the mother who placed her still living son on the cart to the pyre. The speech of the Forty and the speech of the mother are quoted directly, while the tempting words of the ruler are reported indirectly and met by the confession of the Forty.

Here contest means the struggle of martyrdom, crown means the victor reward, confession means public confession of Christian faith, boldness means freedom of speech, company means the united band, and endurance means endurance under suffering. Whole burnt offering means total self offering, while devotion names the devotion for which the martyrs died. Basil uses Septuagint book names and Psalm numbering, so Psalm numbers differ from modern Hebrew numbering, while New Testament citations follow standard modern forms.
