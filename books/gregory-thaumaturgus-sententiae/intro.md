Gregory Thaumaturgus (c. 213–c. 270) wrote these Sentences as a collection of brief moral and theological maxims, apparently preserved as excerpts. They urge piety as the foundation of the virtues and contrast sincere faith with costly but empty religion.

The work opens by insisting that piety must be cultivated above all things, since it is rightly called the mother of the virtues and is their beginning and end. Knowledge of God as the sustaining and creating cause of all things is taught both by sight and by the natural law. The remaining sentences contrast simple, open-air faith with costly piety, and three gathered in the Lord's name with many who deny the divinity.

The work affirms that piety alone is an unchangeable and abiding good, and warns that what is not attainable is somehow not believed because of envy, since to profane souls nothing of good things is credible.
