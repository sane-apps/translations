Amphilochius of Iconium (c. 340–c. 395) wrote this homily for a Christian congregation. He was a fourth-century bishop in Cappadocia, a cousin of Gregory of Nazianzus, and had practiced law in Constantinople before retiring to religious life. The sermon was likely preached at a gathering of believers, perhaps in Caesarea as a guest of Basil, to encourage sinners to run to repentance by showing Christ's mercy to tax collectors and sinners.

The occasion is the Gospel story of Zacchaeus, the chief tax collector of Jericho who climbed a sycamore tree to see Jesus. Amphilochius presents Zacchaeus as a model of repentance, a man trapped by greed and injustice who was called and transformed by Christ. The preacher imagines Zacchaeus's interior thoughts, quotes Jesus's direct address to him, and quotes the murmuring critics who complained that Jesus lodged with a sinner.

A reader should know that the homily uses Septuagint book names and numbering for the Old Testament, so Psalms are cited according to the Septuagint numbering, which differs from the Hebrew in places. The sermon compares the barren spring of Jericho healed by Elisha's salt with the church healed by Christ through the apostles, and draws a parallel between Zacchaeus and Rahab, both saved by receiving Jesus. Key terms include repentance, tax collector, chief tax collector, love for humanity, spring, salt, tree, paradise, the poor, sinner, and sycamore.
