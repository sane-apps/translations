Didymus the Blind (c. 313–c. 398) was a Christian theologian in Alexandria, Egypt, who lost his sight at about four years old and later headed the city's catechetical school. He remained a layman all his life. He wrote this line-by-line commentary on the Book of Job to explain its doctrinal and moral teaching, especially on providence, endurance, and the reasons for the sufferings of the righteous.

The commentary was produced in the fourth century, when Christianity and Rabbinic Judaism were developing distinct interpretive traditions. Didymus reads the Septuagint text of Job, and his exposition covers the first sixteen chapters. The surviving text was preserved in papyrus codices discovered in 1941 near Tura, Egypt, and is one of the earliest line-by-line biblical commentaries from late antiquity to have come down to us.

Didymus presents Job as a divinely approved athlete whose trials are permitted by God not as punishment for sin but to reveal his virtue and refute the devil. He argues that afflictions befall both the wicked and the righteous, but for different reasons: correction for sinners, testing and a crown for the just. The friends of Job, Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, err by assuming only one cause of suffering, namely sin, and therefore attack Job unjustly. Throughout the commentary Didymus defends the immortality of the soul, the resurrection, divine providence over particulars, and the spiritual interpretation of Scripture against literalist and heretical readings. A reader should know that Didymus cites the Septuagint, using its book names and Psalm numbering; where the Septuagint numbering differs from the Hebrew, especially in Psalms, the translation notes the difference. Key terms are rendered as virtue, trial, choice, indifferent things, providence, judgment, courage, endurance, innocence, true, blameless, piety, infliction, adversary, and dispensation.
