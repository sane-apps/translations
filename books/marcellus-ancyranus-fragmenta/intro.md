Marcellus of Ancyra (c. 280–c. 374) was bishop of Ancyra in Galatia and attended the Council of Nicaea in 325. He wrote this treatise to defend himself after opponents he had condemned at Nicaea accused him of unsound doctrine in a letter to Julius, bishop of Rome. The final section is addressed directly to Julius as an appeal to clear his name.

The work responds to Asterius, Eusebius of Caesarea, Narcissus of Neronias, and Paulinus, who taught that the Father and the Word are two distinct realities or substances. Marcellus quotes their own words and argues against them, comparing their ideas to those of Valentinus, Hermes, Marcion, and Plato. He also criticizes the earlier theologian Origen for being influenced by philosophy.

Marcellus insists that the Word of God is eternal, truly God, and inseparable from the Father, not a second distinct reality or a creature. He argues that titles such as 'firstborn of all creation' and 'image of the invisible God' refer to the Word's incarnate plan, not to a pre-cosmic generation. The Word, eternally in the Father, came forth in active power for creation and took flesh for human salvation, and the incarnate kingdom will have an end when all things are subjected to God. The translation renders key terms as Word, unity, distinct reality, plan, power, wisdom, creature, eternity, flesh, proverb, kingdom, and restoration. Scripture citations follow the Septuagint, so Psalm numbers may differ from modern English Bibles, and 'Jesus son of Nave' means Joshua.
