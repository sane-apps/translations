Eustathius of Antioch (c. 320–337) is named in the manuscripts as the author of this treatise, which answers a philosopher who had raised questions about the creation of animals and humanity in Genesis. The work defends the Church's reading of the opening chapters of the Bible, especially the distinct creation of humanity in God's image and likeness and the co-creative role of the Son.

The philosopher's questions prompt Eustathius to explain the difference between the animals and birds formed from the earth in Genesis 2 and those created earlier from the waters. He argues that only the earth-formed creatures are said to be formed by God's own hand, and he offers both a literal reading and an interpretation of them as the nations gathered into the Church. He also addresses the plural words 'let us make' in Genesis 1:26, arguing that they introduce a co-creating person and reveal the shared deity of the Father and the Son.

A reader should know that 'formed' refers to God's direct, hands-on making of humanity and the later animals, as opposed to creation by command. 'In God's image' and 'in God's likeness' describe humanity's role as ruler over the earth. The plural 'let us make' is read as evidence of a co-creator, and the Son is identified as the simple, uncompounded image of God. Bible citations follow modern conventions, with Genesis 1:26–27 and Genesis 2 as the main passages.
