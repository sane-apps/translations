Cyril of Alexandria (c. 376–444) wrote this treatise against Theodore, bishop of Mopsuestia. Cyril was Patriarch of Alexandria from 412 to 444 and became a leading voice in the Christological controversies of his day. He wrote to refute what he saw as a dangerous separation of the divine Word from the human nature in Christ.

Theodore of Mopsuestia was bishop of Mopsuestia in Cilicia for about 36 years and died in 428. He had been a pupil of Diodore, and his early arguments against Arians and Apollinarians led him to speak of the Incarnation as though it were only a condescension of God the Son connecting with himself a man who already had a distinct being.

In these fragments Cyril urges his opponent to grasp the depth of the mystery of Christ. He insists that while divinity and flesh are distinct in their natural properties, they must be confessed as one Son in an inseparable union. The human nature in Christ is honored and sanctified, yet as the Word he is the giver of the Spirit and did not need baptism or the Spirit for his own being. A reader should know that "divinity" means the divine nature of the Word, "flesh" means Christ's human nature, and "consubstantial" means sharing the same substance or nature.
