Didymus the Blind (c. 313–c. 398) was a fourth-century Alexandrian theologian and biblical commentator. Blind since childhood, he remained a layman all his life and headed the influential catechetical school of Alexandria. He was a student of Origen and a loyal follower of his teachings, and a leading opponent of Arianism. Because his works were later condemned for their association with Origen's teachings, most of his writings were not copied during the Middle Ages and were lost.

These fragments come from Didymus's commentary on Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians, focusing on chapters 15 and 16. He wrote to refute those in Corinth who said the soul is perishable and bodily resurrection superfluous, and later heretics such as Marcionites, those who say only the soul is made alive, and those who posit a fifth substance. The commentary responds to beliefs and concerns arising from the Pauline text itself.

A reader will meet Paul, whose words are quoted and expounded, and Christ, whose death and resurrection Didymus argues guarantee the resurrection of all people. He also argues against heretics and doubters, including Marcionites who baptize living people on behalf of unbaptized dead people, and those who offer alternative interpretations of Paul's words. The reader should know that Didymus follows the Septuagint for Old Testament quotations, and that terms like resurrection, soul, natural body, spiritual body, and incorruptibility are used to distinguish the perishable body that is sown from the transformed risen body.
