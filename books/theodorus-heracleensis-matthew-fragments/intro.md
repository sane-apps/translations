Theodore of Heraclea (c. 335–355) was a bishop in Thrace and a leading member of the Eusebian party that opposed Athanasius of Alexandria. He wrote this commentary on the Gospel of Matthew for Christian readers who needed help understanding difficult passages and apparent disagreements between the evangelists. They are not the fragments of Apollinaris of Laodicea. The surviving fragments come from a larger work that has otherwise been lost, preserved through later collections of quotations from early Christian writers.

The commentary moves through Matthew from the opening genealogy to the passion. Theodore often defends Matthew's consistency with Luke, especially in the infancy narrative, by distinguishing different times and circumstances. A reader will meet the evangelists Matthew, Luke, and Mark, as well as John the Baptist, Herod, David, Abraham, Moses, and Beelzebul. Theodore quotes Jesus's sayings from Matthew, sometimes without explicit markers, and he also quotes and argues against other interpreters, introduced with phrases like 'others say' or 'another interpreter says.' He cites the Jewish historian Josephus as a witness to John the Baptist.

A reader should know that 'kingdom of heaven' means Christ's presence and its benefits, and that 'weeds' in the parable are heresies that resemble true Christianity. 'Devotion' translates the Greek word for piety toward God, the goal for which believers must endure loss and conflict. Theodore uses the old Greek names for Bible books and follows the Psalm numbering of the Greek Old Testament, which sometimes differs from the Hebrew numbering found in many modern Bibles. The commentary also shows how a mid-fourth-century exegete used terms like 'person' and 'substance' in theological disputes of his time.
